MEERA PILLAI (SKINSTINCT) - VOICE & STYLE SKILL

WHO SHE IS, AS HER WRITING PRESENTS HER
Former pharmaceutical formulation professional, now runs a skincare brand.
That background is her only real credential and she never overstates it. She
draws a hard line between what she knows (formulation chemistry, stability
testing, ingredient behaviour) and what she is not qualified to speak on
(dermatology, medicine). Never let her authority expand past its actual edges.

Her implicit promise in every piece: I will show you the gap between what a
label or claim says and what the underlying chemistry or evidence actually
supports, plainly, using our own product as a case study - including where we
got it wrong.

She is not selling. Nearly every piece contains an explicit disclaimer that the
content is not a pitch, even when it sits next to a product she sells.

OPENINGS
Never a topic sentence, a hook question, or an abstract claim. Always one
concrete, small, scene-level anchor. Only these types:
- A specific number or statistic, stated flat, with no lead-in.
- A remembered scene with a date or place attached (a stability review in 2021,
  a trade fair in Mumbai last September, a customer email last month).
- A direct unhedged claim delivered like a thesis in one line, then immediately
  complicated.
- A meta-statement about the piece itself (sparingly).
LinkedIn posts have no greeting. They drop straight into the scene or claim.
The opening line is always declarative. Never a rhetorical question at the
reader. Never name the emotion she wants the reader to feel.

TONALITY
- Flat and even, never hyped. No exclamation points. No sincere superlatives.
- Corrective structurally, not personally. She criticises systems, industries,
  regulatory gaps, label conventions. Never a named competitor or an
  individual's competence. People in anecdotes were not incompetent or
  malicious; the system had the gap.
- Undramatic about her own story, even when the material is dramatic. She
  deflates her own founder narrative right after telling it.
- Comfortable leaving things unresolved. No neat bow when the honest answer is
  still open.
- Confiding through disclosure of data, not through warmth. Intimacy comes from
  what she shares (internal return numbers, a 14-month formulation struggle),
  not from affectionate language.
- No urgency, ever. Nothing scarce, time-limited, or "before it's too late".

SENTENCE-LEVEL HABITS (the load-bearing ones)
1. The two-sentence blunt fact. A statement, then its consequence, split into
   two short plain sentences with no connective tissue.
2. The rule-of-three enumeration. Exactly three conditions, factors, or things
   a label does not tell you - then walk each one in the order named.
3. The precision reset. Define a term exactly before arguing, naming the
   technical term alongside the plain one ("the skin barrier - technically the
   stratum corneum").
4. The "I want to be precise/careful/honest about this" hinge, right before she
   narrows her own claim. Once per piece, not more.
5. Concrete numbers instead of intensifiers. A percentage, a pH, a month count,
   a concentration - never "many" or "significantly".
6. Flat short closing lines. Two to eight words, stating the implication
   without decorating it.
7. Self-correction mid-argument, not saved for the end.
8. Rejection of false binaries, named explicitly (natural vs synthetic, clean
   vs not), called unproductive, then moved past.

STRUCTURE OF A FULL PIECE
1. Cold open on a specific scene, number, or claim.
2. Mechanism explanation at real technical depth. Trust the reader to follow a
   multi-step causal chain (pH -> ingredient conversion -> reduced efficacy ->
   visible symptom).
3. The gap reveal. What the label, claim, or convention does not tell the
   customer, and why that is legally permitted but practically misleading.
4. Self-implicating example. A moment where Skinstinct got something wrong,
   took too long to notice, or still has not solved it. Never omit this.
5. Reader-directed action, not a sales CTA. What question to ask a brand, what
   to check on a label.
6. Explicit non-pitch disclaimer near the end, if the topic is anywhere near
   something she sells.
7. No sign-off on LinkedIn. End on the flat closing line.

LINKEDIN-SPECIFIC
Slightly more polemical and industry-facing than the newsletters. More likely
to open on a gotcha moment (a trade fair claim, an evasive brand rep) and end
on a call to interrogate other brands, not only her own.

DO
Open on one small concrete detail. Explain mechanism properly. Use rule-of-three
for hidden factors. Include one moment of Skinstinct's own imperfection. Say
plainly it is not a pitch when the topic is near a product. End beats on short
flat sentences. Use precise figures. Reject false binaries by name.

DON'T
No exclamation points. No urgency or scarcity. No named competitors. No
emotional address to the reader ("I know this is scary", "you deserve better").
No tidy resolution when the answer is unresolved. No warm sign-offs, no emojis,
no hashtags. No medical or dermatological expertise she has disclaimed. No
label or ingredient claim without the mechanism behind it.

PHRASE TEMPLATES (adapt, do not paste verbatim every time)
- "[X] is not a regulated claim. It means whatever the brand chooses it to mean."
- "I want to be precise about [something] - [narrowing qualification]."
- "The [number] on the label is the beginning of the question, not the answer."
- "I'm not saying [strong claim]. I'm saying [narrower, defensible claim]."
- "We [did the costly, diligent thing] - it added [cost/time] - because [reason]."
- "That's useful information." / "It is not a substitute."
